# Gold example — a shipped 30-second commercial

A deadpan product film for a pizza brand: a scientist puts a cheeseburger on the floor of a hangar laboratory, a colossal road roller drives over it, and behind the machine the burger has become a cheeseburger pizza.

Seven references: male scientist sheet, female scientist sheet, hangar location, burger in a kraft carton, pizza in a kraft box, road roller, logo.

Read this for **density, tone and section behaviour**. Do not copy its content into an unrelated brief — the specific locks here are a scar record of this film's failures, not a style guide.

The scientists lift it onto a lab bench, document everything, analyze a pickle under a lamp, and the film closes on a hero product shot with the brand logo.

LIGHTING PROGRESSION
The film runs through four scripted light states in order. Each state is a different set of dominant sources, not a mood word, and each segment is tagged with the state it plays in. The states never run out of order and never blend.

LIGHT STATE A — CLINICAL, S1 to S3. Cold white daylight from the deep doorway and the clerestory skylight bands. Flat, even, unremarkable, top-down. Long cool reflections on the polished concrete. The amber wall strip and gooseneck lamp are present but visually irrelevant. No warmth touches the subject. This is the boring baseline the rest of the film departs from.

LIGHT STATE B — ALARM, S4 to S7. Triggered on the button press, instantly, not gradually. The overhead strips drop to half and the skylights read dimmer. The roller's amber beacon becomes the dominant moving source in the room: hard turning bars of amber sweeping across the floor, the drums and the scientists' faces at a slow steady rhythm, throwing long rotating shadows. Shadows deepen, the teal pushes colder, contrast climbs. Faces are lit in alternating amber and shadow as the beacon passes.

LIGHT STATE C — FLOOD, S8 to S9. The light event of the film. As the roller creeps away and its shadow slides off the box, full cold daylight from the doorway floods back at once and the beacon recedes with the machine. A single broad warm shaft from the doorway sweeps across the pizza as the shadow clears, steam glowing white inside it, a soft reflection blooming on the concrete. Brightest and widest state in the film. Strictly realistic — no glow effects, no magic, no bloom filter.

LIGHT STATE D — PRODUCT, S10 to S16. Warmest and most controlled. The gooseneck lamp over the analysis bench becomes the key: a warm hard raking light from camera-right, low across the toppings, catching glints on the melted cheese. Cold daylight is demoted to a thin cool rim on the back edge of the crust and the box lid. The hangar behind falls away into dark soft bokeh. ## What to notice

**The geometry lock is a quarter of the prompt.** That is proportionate. It is the section that took three versions to get right and it is the one that decides whether the film reads as one continuous event or a pile of shots.

**Locks answer specific past failures.** `no other clipboard colour` exists because a generation produced red ones. `never a deep squat` exists because the model kept crouching. `do not re-centre it` exists because the model kept improving the composition. Locks are a scar record, not a style guide — do not carry these particular ones into an unrelated brief.

**Segments are numbered and referenced by ID everywhere** — in OPTICS, in the acceleration lock, in the light states, in POSITIVE LOCKS. That cross-referencing is what lets one line at the bottom govern a specific moment four sections above.

**Everything critical appears twice.** The clean floor is in LOCATION MAP, in PHYSICS and in POSITIVE LOCKS. The slow speed is in CAMERA as SPEED LOCK and again in the locks. The anchors are in GEOMETRY LOCK, inside every segment that uses them, and again in the locks.

**Timings run to one decimal and every gap is filled** with an explicit cut marker, so the model is never left to invent a transition.

**The two riskiest lines in the film are the sauce pour and the cheese pull** — the two product shots where a tool enters frame. Both have a cheap downgrade to a passive macro that keeps the shot count at six. Know which of your shots are the fragile ones before you batch.
